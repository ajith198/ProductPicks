Once you've got a mat, blocks and a strap are the next things most beginners end up needing — not because a teacher told you to buy props, but because certain poses are genuinely out of reach without a little extra height or length under your hands. A block closes the gap between your palm and the floor in a half-moon pose; a strap closes the gap between your hands and your feet in a seated forward fold. Neither is a crutch — plenty of experienced practitioners keep using them for years. We haven't personally tested every item listed here — this is based on published testing and buying guides from outlets like Yoga Journal, OutdoorGearLab, and hands-on strap-sizing guides from prop makers like Hugger Mugger, and we'll flag anywhere we're relying on that research rather than hands-on use.

## What Actually Matters in Blocks and Straps

### Block Material: Foam vs. Cork

The two materials you'll actually see are **EVA foam** and **cork**. Foam blocks are lighter, cheaper, and more forgiving under the hands and knees, which makes them the easier starting point for most beginners — a standard foam block weighs well under a pound. The tradeoff is that foam compresses and dents over years of heavy use, especially if you lean your full body weight into it regularly. Cork blocks don't compress and grip a wooden or hardwood floor noticeably better than foam does, since foam can slide when you push against it at an angle. Cork is also heavier and firmer against the body, so it feels harder under sensitive knees or wrists in longer holds. Wood blocks exist too but are rare now — cork gives similar firmness with less weight.

### Block Size

Nearly every block on the market is the same standard footprint: roughly **4 x 6 x 9 inches**. The block's three different edge widths (4", 6", and 9") are the actual point — resting the block on its different sides gives you three height options from one prop, so you can start low and work up as a pose requires more lift. There's little reason to shop on size; the real decision is material and how many you buy (most routines call for two).

### Strap Buckle Type

Straps come with either a **D-ring buckle** or a **cinch buckle**. D-ring buckles — two metal rings the strap threads through — are generally easier for beginners to adjust precisely and hold a loop securely once set. Cinch buckles adjust faster with one hand mid-pose, which some practitioners prefer once they're used to it, but a cheap plastic cinch can slip loose under load; a metal cinch buckle is more reliable than a plastic one if you go that route.

### Strap Length

Straps are sold in **6, 8, and 10-foot** lengths. Six feet is enough for most people under about 5'6" doing basic seated stretches and simple binds. Eight feet is the safer default for most adults between roughly 5'4" and 6'0", since a too-short strap defeats the purpose in deeper stretches. Taller practitioners, or anyone planning to use a strap for assisted backbends or shoulder openers (not just seated folds), should size up to 10 feet for the extra working length.

## A Few Things to Verify Before You Buy

- **Buy two blocks, not one** — most sequences that call for a block at all call for a pair, one per hand.
- **Measure your height against the strap-length guide** above rather than guessing; an 8-foot strap that's too short defeats the point in a deep stretch.
- **Check the buckle material specifically** on any cinch-style strap — plastic cinches are the most common point of failure under load, metal cinches hold up better.
- **Cork blocks are noticeably heavier** than foam — fine for home use, less convenient if you're carrying props to a studio on foot or by bike.
- **Look up the current price directly on the retailer's page** — these fluctuate with sales more than the approximate figures above suggest.
