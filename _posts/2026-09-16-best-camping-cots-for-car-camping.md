A cot solves two problems a sleeping pad on the ground never fully does: it gets you up off cold, uneven, or wet ground, and it keeps your sleeping bag or pad from picking up condensation and dirt. For car camping specifically — where packed size and weight matter far less than they do for backpacking — a cot is one of the highest-value comfort upgrades you can make to a campsite. But "camping cot" covers everything from a $70 steel-frame folder to a $400 ultralight backpacking cot, and the right one depends heavily on how you're actually using it. Here's what to look at, and a few picks across price points and use cases.

## What actually matters

**Frame material and weight capacity.** Steel-frame cots are heavier (15-25+ lbs) but cheap and sturdy, typically rated for 250-300 lbs — plenty for most car camping. Aluminum-frame cots cut weight dramatically (often under 5 lbs) at a much higher price, and are the standard for backpacking cots where every pound matters. Check the stated weight capacity against your actual needs, not just the "sleeps comfortably" marketing language — it varies more between models than you'd expect.

**Elevated vs. low-profile design.** Traditional elevated cots (14-18 inches off the ground) give you storage space underneath for gear bins or boots, and are easier to get in and out of. Low-profile cots (a few inches off the ground) fit inside smaller tents where headroom is tight, and feel more stable if you tend to roll around at night, but you lose the under-cot storage.

**Setup mechanism.** Cots range from simple X-frame steel legs that unfold and lock, to pole-and-fabric systems similar to camp chairs that use shock-corded segments and a taut suspension. The pole-and-fabric type (common on lighter, pricier cots) is faster to set up solo but has more small parts that can be lost or damaged; steel X-frames are nearly foolproof but heavier and bulkier when packed.

**Packed size, for actual car camping.** Even though weight isn't a huge constraint when you're not carrying it on your back, packed length matters for trunk space — many steel cots pack down to 40+ inches long, which is awkward in a smaller car. If trunk space is tight, check the folded dimensions, not just the weight.

**Sleeping surface and noise.** Cheaper cots use a taut fabric deck alone, which some people find fine and others find too firm or "hammock-like" if the tension sags over a season of use. Mid-range and premium cots often add a built-in foam pad or coil-suspension layer for a mattress-like feel. Fabric decks can also creak or squeak with movement — worth checking reviews for this if you're a light sleeper.

**Height for getting in and out.** If you have knee or back issues, or are sharing a tent with young kids who need to climb up, an 18-inch elevated cot is much easier to use than a low-profile one — factor this in alongside storage space underneath.

## Verify before you buy

- Confirm current price on the retailer's listing — cot pricing moves around with seasonal sales more than most gear categories.
- Double-check the folded/packed dimensions against your trunk or storage space if you're not backpacking-weight-constrained but are space-constrained.
- Check the stated max user height and weight capacity against your own, not just the "supports up to" headline number, since sleeping surface length varies by model.
- If buying a lightweight pole-and-fabric cot, look at what's covered under warranty for the tensioning/lock hardware — these are the parts most likely to wear out first.
