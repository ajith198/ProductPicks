A sleeping bag gets most of the attention when people plan their first backpacking trip, but the pad underneath it does two jobs that matter just as much: it insulates you from the ground, which pulls heat away from your body far faster than cold air does, and it's the difference between actually sleeping and just lying awake on rocks and roots. Beginners often assume any pad will do and end up shivering on a warm-rated bag because the ground stole all their heat, or they overspend on features they don't need. The category boils down to two real decisions — how much insulation you need and whether you want foam or inflatable comfort — and getting those right matters more than any single brand name.

## What actually matters

**R-value is the number that actually predicts whether you'll be warm, and it's simpler than it looks.** R-value measures a pad's resistance to heat loss into the ground, and unlike sleeping bag temperature ratings, R-values from different brands are now standardized (ASTM F3340) and roughly comparable across manufacturers. A pad in the 1-2 range is a three-season-in-name-only summer pad — fine for warm nights, cold on anything below the 50s. Roughly 3-4.5 covers most three-season backpacking down into the 30s, and anything above 5 starts moving into winter territory. R-values are also additive, so a common trick for cold trips is stacking a closed-cell foam pad under an inflatable one rather than buying a separate winter-specific pad.

**Foam and inflatable pads solve the same problem in very different ways, and the trade-off is comfort versus bulk and reliability.** Closed-cell foam pads (the accordion-folded or rolled kind) can't puncture, need no setup, and double as an emergency seat or ground cloth — but they're thin, less cushioned, and bulkier to carry strapped to the outside of a pack. Inflatable pads pack down small and are dramatically more comfortable since you can dial in firmness by how much air you add, but they can puncture (most ship with a repair kit) and take a minute or two of blowing or pumping each night. A lot of experienced backpackers actually carry a thin foam pad as a lightweight backup and sit pad even when their main pad is inflatable.

**Pump sacks and built-in pumps solve the "out of breath at altitude" problem, but they add weight.** Manually blowing up a pad at elevation after a long hiking day is more tiring than it sounds, and moist breath introduced into the pad over time can encourage mildew. A separate pump sack (often included) or an integrated hand-pump baffle avoids both problems and inflates a pad in well under a minute — worth prioritizing over saving an ounce or two if you can.

**Packed size and weight decide whether a pad actually rides well on a backpacking-sized pack, not just a car-camping trip.** A pad that's excellent on paper but takes up a third of your pack volume becomes a daily annoyance. Ultralight inflatable pads typically pack down to roughly the size of a water bottle, while budget inflatables and foam pads are noticeably bulkier — worth checking packed dimensions against your actual pack size before buying, especially for a smaller backpacking pack.

## Before you buy: a quick checklist

1. **Match R-value to your coldest expected night**, not the average one — the low temperature on a forecast, not the daytime high, is what your pad has to handle.
2. **Budget for a pump sack** if the pad doesn't include one — it saves real effort at the end of a long hiking day and keeps moisture out of the pad's insulation.
3. **Check packed size against your actual pack**, especially for smaller backpacking packs, since packed volume varies a lot between models.
4. **Confirm the repair kit is included** on any inflatable pad, and keep it in an easy-to-find spot in your pack rather than buried at the bottom.
5. **Confirm current price and R-value on the listing before buying**, since pricing shifts often and some models are sold in multiple R-value or size variants — the ranges above are approximate at the time of writing.

None of this needs to be complicated: figure out the coldest night you're realistically planning for, decide how much comfort-versus-weight trade-off you're willing to make, and pick accordingly. Get the R-value right for your trips and almost any reputable pad in the category will do its one real job — keeping the ground from stealing the warmth your sleeping bag is trying to give you.
