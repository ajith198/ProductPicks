A hammock looks like the simplest piece of gear in a backpacker's kit — a piece of fabric and two ends — but the category has more real trade-offs than it first appears. Beginners often buy the hammock and forget that suspension straps are usually sold separately, then end up wrapping cheap rope around a tree and either damaging the bark or watching the hammock slowly slide down the trunk all night. Others buy a single-size hammock expecting to lounge diagonally like they saw online, only to find it's too narrow to lie flat in. None of this is complicated once you know what to look for, and a decent hammock setup can replace a tent for warm-weather trips where hanging spots are available.

## What actually matters

**Suspension straps matter more than the hammock fabric itself, and they're frequently sold separately.** Wide, flat tree-friendly straps (as opposed to thin rope or webbing) spread the load across more bark surface area and are required outright in many U.S. state and national park campgrounds and forests specifically to prevent tree damage. Straps with multiple stitched loops let you adjust the hang angle without retying knots each time, which matters because the ideal hang is a moderate sag — pulled too tight and the fabric puts uneven pressure on your body; too loose and you sink into an uncomfortable U-shape. If a hammock's listed price doesn't mention straps, budget an extra $12-$40 for a separate pair, since some budget hammocks include basic straps and others don't.

**Single vs. double sizing is really about how you sleep in it, not how many people you plan to fit.** A "double" hammock (roughly 78-90 inches wide) isn't primarily for two people — it's wide enough for one person to lie at a diagonal angle across the fabric, which flattens out the body and is significantly more comfortable for actual sleeping than lying straight down a narrow single hammock. Single hammocks (usually 50-60 inches wide) save weight and pack size and work fine for casual lounging or short breaks, but reviewers consistently note that taller or heavier users find singles too tight for a full night's sleep. If the hammock is your primary sleeping system rather than a yard accessory, size up to a double even if you're backpacking solo.

**Fabric weight and weave decide the pack-size-versus-durability trade-off.** Budget hammocks are typically 210D (denier) parachute nylon — tough, abrasion-resistant, and cheap, but heavier and bulkier when packed. Ultralight backpacking hammocks use thinner 20D-40D ripstop nylon, cutting weight dramatically (some single ultralight models weigh close to 10 ounces all-in) while still holding a few hundred pounds of rated capacity, at a real cost in price and a slightly higher risk of snags from sharp objects like zippers or pocket knives left in a pocket.

**A bug net and rainfly turn a hammock from a lounging accessory into an actual overnight shelter, and they're not always included.** For an afternoon nap in the backyard, a bare hammock is fine. For an overnight trip, mosquitoes and dew make a built-in or add-on bug net close to mandatory in most regions for at least part of the season, and a separate ridgeline-mounted rainfly is what actually makes hanging overnight viable if rain is even a possibility. Buying the hammock, straps, bug net, and rainfly as one integrated system (rather than mixing brands) generally fits together more reliably than assembling pieces separately.

## Before you buy: a quick checklist

1. **Confirm whether straps are included** on the specific listing — this is the single most common reason buyers end up paying more than the sticker price implies.
2. **Check your local land manager's rules** — many U.S. national forests, state parks, and some private campgrounds require wide tree-friendly straps and prohibit rope or narrow webbing to protect bark.
3. **Size up to a double if the hammock is your primary sleep system**, even solo, so you can lie on the diagonal rather than straight down the fabric.
4. **Decide up front whether you need a bug net and rainfly**, and check whether the brand sells matching accessories before mixing hardware from different systems.
5. **Confirm current price, weight, and included accessories on the listing before buying**, since hammock pricing and what's bundled in the box shifts often across color variants and promotions — the figures above are approximate at the time of writing.

None of this needs to be complicated: figure out whether you're lounging for an afternoon or sleeping overnight, size and accessorize accordingly, and don't skip real tree-friendly straps. Get those two things right and almost any reputable hammock on this list will do its one real job — getting you off the ground and comfortably swaying between two trees.
