Shop for an air purifier and you'll see a wall of marketing language — "medical-grade," "99.97%," "smart sensors" — that makes it hard to compare models on anything concrete. The numbers that actually matter are simpler than the marketing suggests: CADR (Clean Air Delivery Rate) tells you how fast a unit actually cleans air, and that number has to be matched against your room size, not just glanced at. This guide focuses on what those specs mean in practice and which models deliver on them at different price points, without making any claims about health outcomes — that's between you and your doctor, not a buying guide.

## What actually matters

**CADR is the single most useful spec, and it's measured in cubic feet per minute for dust, smoke, and pollen separately.** A purifier's CADR rating (ideally AHAM-verified, an independent certification) tells you how much filtered air it actually delivers, not just how big its filter is. As a rule of thumb, look for a CADR that's at least two-thirds of your room's square footage for a reasonable number of air changes per hour — a unit with a 130 CADR in a 500 sq ft living room will run constantly and still underperform compared to the same unit in a 150 sq ft bedroom.

**Room coverage numbers on the box are often optimistic, so treat them as a ceiling, not a target.** Manufacturers typically list coverage based on a set number of air changes per hour (often around 4-5), calculated from the CADR. Buying a unit rated for exactly your room's square footage means it's working at the edge of its ability; sizing up one tier gives you quieter operation on lower fan speeds and more headroom.

**True HEPA (or H13) filtration is the baseline worth insisting on, and it's a physical filter, not a coating or ionizer claim.** True HEPA filters are independently rated to capture 99.97% of particles down to 0.3 microns — dust, pollen, pet dander, and smoke particles all fall in or above that range. Ionizers and "PlasmaWave"-style ozone-adjacent technologies can be a nice-to-have add-on, but they shouldn't substitute for a real HEPA filter as the primary filtration stage.

**Noise level matters more for bedroom placement than almost any other spec.** A unit that's fine in a living room can be genuinely disruptive next to a bed. Look for a published low-speed dB rating — anything around 20-25 dB on its lowest setting is close to silent, while many purifiers climb into the 50s dB range on higher fan speeds needed to hit their rated CADR in a larger room.

**Filter replacement cost is the real long-term price of the machine, not the sticker price.** Most true HEPA filters need replacing every 6-12 months, and annual filter costs range from roughly $30 on affordable models to $80+ on some premium units. A cheaper purifier with expensive proprietary filters can cost more over three years than a pricier unit with a longer-lasting or washable pre-filter — factor this in before buying on sticker price alone.

## Before you buy: a quick checklist

1. **Measure your room and compare it against the CADR-based coverage rating, not just the square footage on the box** — buying one size up gives you quieter, more effective operation.
2. **Check the published low-speed dB rating if it's going in a bedroom** — a loud purifier gets turned off or unplugged.
3. **Confirm the filter is true HEPA (or H13) and look up the annual filter replacement cost before buying**, not just the purifier's sticker price.
4. **Don't rely on marketing language about health outcomes** — look at independent CADR/AHAM certification and filter specs instead.
5. **Confirm current price on the listing before buying** — air purifier pricing swings substantially with sales, and the ranges above are approximate at the time of writing.

There's no single "best" air purifier — the right pick depends on your room size and how much you're willing to spend upfront versus on replacement filters over time. Start by matching CADR to your actual square footage, then let noise level and budget narrow the choice between the compact Levoit, the tiny Blueair, the value-oriented Winix, or the higher-capacity Coway.
