A snow shovel looks like the simplest purchase on this entire site, and most of the time it is — until the first real storm hits and you're standing in the driveway with a shovel that's either too heavy to lift a full scoop of wet snow, or too narrow to clear anything faster than one driveway-length per ice age. The right shovel depends mostly on what you're actually clearing (a short walkway vs. a long two-car driveway), what kind of snow your area usually gets (light powder vs. heavy wet snow), and how much you want to protect your back while doing it.

## What actually matters

**Push shovels and scoop shovels solve different problems, and a lot of mid-range shovels now combine both.** A push shovel (sometimes just called a "pusher") has a wide, mostly flat blade designed to shove snow sideways off a flat surface — driveways, sidewalks, patios. A scoop shovel has a more curved, cupped blade meant for lifting and throwing snow, which you need for piling snow higher, clearing stairs, or digging out a buried car. Many popular combo blades split the difference with a slightly curved push-scoop shape that handles both jobs reasonably well, which is the right call for most single-family homes that don't have a huge amount of snow to move.

**Blade material is a real trade-off, not just a price difference.** Poly (plastic) blades are light, glide over pavement without scratching it, and don't rust, but a cheap poly blade can crack if you're chipping at hardened ice rather than pushing fresh snow. Steel blades — or steel edge/wear strips bolted onto a poly blade — scrape down to bare pavement more effectively and hold up better against packed or icy snow, but add noticeable weight and can dent or curl over time. The combination most review sites and hardware stores land on as the best all-around choice for homeowners is a **poly blade with a steel wear strip**: light enough to swing for an hour, tough enough to scrape ice.

**Bent/ergonomic shafts are a genuine benefit for most people, though reviewers disagree on how large it is.** A curved or kinked shaft lets you keep your back straighter and push more with your legs and arms instead of bending forward repeatedly, which is the motion most associated with shoveling-related back strain. Some review outlets call it the single most important feature on a shovel; others find the difference modest compared to just using good form (short scoops, pushing rather than lifting, bending at the knees). Treat it as a real plus worth paying a little extra for, not a guarantee against a sore back.

**For long or wide driveways, a wheeled snow pusher is worth the extra size and cost.** These have a wide angled blade (commonly 29-48 inches) mounted on a wheeled frame, so you push the whole unit forward on its wheels instead of lifting anything — the snow curls off to one side as you walk. They clear a driveway dramatically faster than a standard shovel and put less strain on your back, but they're bulky to store, overkill for a short walkway, and genuinely only shine on fresh, unpacked snow rather than deep drifts or solid ice.

## Before you buy: a quick checklist

1. **Match the blade type to your job** — a flat push blade for clearing flat surfaces, a curved scoop for lifting/piling, or a combo blade if you need both.
2. **Check whether the blade has a steel edge or wear strip**, not just poly — this matters a lot more if your area gets icy or heavy wet snow rather than light powder.
3. **Consider a bent/ergonomic shaft if you have any history of back problems**, and use good form (short pushes, bending at the knees) regardless of which shovel you buy.
4. **Measure your driveway width before buying a wheeled snow pusher** — they're excellent for long, wide spaces but impractical to store and maneuver for a short walkway.
5. **Verify current price and blade width on the listing**, since snow-shovel pricing and availability swing noticeably with the first cold snap of the season.

There's no single "best" snow shovel — the right one depends on how much area you're clearing, what kind of snow you typically get, and how much you want to spend on ergonomics. For most homeowners, a bent-shaft combo shovel like the AMES True Temper covers the majority of winters; budget poly pushers like the True Temper or Garant are fine for light snow; and a wheeled pusher like the Garvee earns its keep on longer driveways once you're tired of lifting anything at all.
