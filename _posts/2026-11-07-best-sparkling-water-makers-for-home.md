A home soda maker turns tap water into carbonated water in seconds, which sounds like a small convenience until you add up how many cans or bottles of seltzer a household goes through in a year — both in cost and in recycling. The category runs from roughly $50 budget machines up to $250+ premium designs, and the price spread buys you build quality, bottle material, and sometimes the ability to carbonate drinks beyond plain water, rather than a fundamentally different fizz. Nearly every machine in this space uses a replaceable CO2 cylinder as its only ongoing cost, so the real decision isn't just which machine looks nice on your counter — it's which CO2 format you're committing to and how easy that format is to refill or exchange near you.

## What actually matters

**CO2 cylinder format determines your long-term cost and convenience more than the machine itself.** SodaStream uses its own proprietary "Quick Connect" cylinders, which is both the brand's biggest strength and its biggest lock-in: exchanges are widely available at major retailers (Walmart, Target, Bed Bath & Beyond in the US), so running out is rarely a real problem, but you're tied to SodaStream's exchange network and pricing. Drinkmate and Aarke, by contrast, use standard-threaded CO2 cylinders that are also compatible with paintball and homebrew-supply refill shops, which can be cheaper and more flexible if you have access to one nearby — but less universally available than a SodaStream exchange rack at your local big-box store. Check what's actually convenient to refill in your area before you buy into either ecosystem.

**Electric vs. manual carbonation is mostly a matter of feel, not fizz quality.** Manual machines (most SodaStream models, Aarke, Drinkmate) use a lever or button you press to release CO2 into the bottle, and you control carbonation level by how many times you press it — simple, works without power, and lets you dial in exactly how fizzy you want each batch. A newer generation of electric/automatic machines does this at the push of a single button with a consistent, repeatable result, which is nice if you want zero guesswork but is one more thing that could eventually fail electronically. Neither approach makes noticeably better sparkling water; it's a preference about control and reliability.

**Bottle material and dishwasher-safety vary more than people expect.** Most machines ship with BPA-free plastic bottles that need to be hand-washed (the pressure-rated plastic generally isn't dishwasher-safe), while some premium options support glass or stainless bottles for drinks you plan to serve right away rather than store carbonated. If a dedicated bottle gets damaged or lost, replacement bottles are brand- and model-specific and not always cheap — factor that into the real cost of ownership, especially for a multi-bottle household.

**"Carbonates anything" is a genuinely useful feature, but only for specific machines.** Standard SodaStream and most entry machines are designed for plain water only — carbonating juice or wine voids the mechanism's intent and can gunk up the carbonating tube over time. Drinkmate's OmniFizz and Breville's InFizz Fusion are built specifically to carbonate juice, wine, cocktails, and other drinks via a different cap/valve design that doesn't backflow liquid into the CO2 line. If you only want sparkling water, this feature is irrelevant; if you're excited about carbonated juice or homemade sodas with syrup, it's worth paying for.

## A simple decision framework

- **Just want cheap, reliable plain sparkling water** → SodaStream Terra.
- **Want the Terra's simplicity but prefer a glass bottle and retro look** → SodaStream ART.
- **Want to carbonate juice, wine, or cocktails, not just water, without overspending** → Drinkmate OmniFizz.
- **Want "carbonate anything" in a more premium, design-led package** → Breville InFizz Fusion.
- **Want the nicest-looking machine on the counter and don't need anything but water** → Aarke Carbonator 3.

## Before you buy: a quick checklist

1. **Check CO2 refill/exchange availability near you first** — SodaStream's exchange network is the most widely available in big-box stores; Drinkmate/Aarke-style threaded cylinders open up paintball/homebrew refill shops but aren't guaranteed to be nearby.
2. **Decide if you actually want to carbonate anything besides water** — it determines whether a water-only machine (SodaStream, Aarke) or a "carbonates anything" machine (Drinkmate, Breville) is the right category for you.
3. **Factor in whether the CO2 cylinder ships included** — several premium machines, including the Breville, are commonly sold with the cylinder as a separate purchase.
4. **Confirm bottle care requirements** — most pressure-rated bottles are hand-wash only, and replacements are brand-specific.
5. **Confirm current price on the listing before buying** — soda-maker pricing moves with sales and bundles, and the ranges above are approximate at the time of writing.

There's no single "best" soda maker — the right one depends on whether you just want cheap, reliable seltzer or you're after a machine that can carbonate more than water. Any of the five picks above will reliably turn tap water into sparkling water: start from how committed you are to a CO2 refill ecosystem and whether "carbonate anything" is a feature you'll actually use, and let that decide between the budget SodaStream Terra, the retro SodaStream ART, the versatile Drinkmate OmniFizz, the premium Breville InFizz Fusion, or the all-metal Aarke Carbonator 3.
