Most apartment leases ban open flames, propane tanks, and charcoal on balconies or in shared outdoor space — but an electric grill plugged into a regular wall outlet is almost always fine, indoors or on a balcony, because it produces no open flame and no combustion exhaust. That's made indoor electric grills one of the more practical small-kitchen upgrades for anyone who wants seared meat, grill marks, or a hot panini without a fire code violation. The category ranges from $20 two-serving contact grills to $150+ multi-function griddlers, and the differences that actually matter for apartment living are less about wattage on the spec sheet and more about how much smoke and grease spatter each design actually produces, how much counter space it demands, and whether it can do more than one job.

## What actually matters

**"Smokeless" describes a design category, not a guarantee of zero smoke.** Grills marketed as smokeless (like George Foreman's open-grate line) work by tilting the cooking surface so fat and juices drain away from the heating element into a separate tray, instead of pooling on a flat plate and burning into smoke the way a classic contact grill can with fatty foods. This meaningfully reduces smoke for burgers, steak, and bacon compared to a flat contact grill, but it doesn't eliminate it — searing very fatty cuts at high heat will still produce some smoke on any electric grill, and a nearby window or range hood fan is still worth using if you're grilling something greasy.

**Contact grills (two heated plates that close on the food) cook faster but only fit food thin enough to be pressed.** This is the classic George Foreman shape: it's fast because both sides cook at once, and it doubles as a panini press or sandwich grill, but a thick bone-in chicken thigh or a tall burger won't close properly between the plates. Open-grate or single-flat-surface grills (Hamilton Beach's searing grill line, the "full grill" mode on a Cuisinart griddler) cook one side at a time like an outdoor grill, which takes longer but handles food of any thickness and gives more realistic grill marks and searing.

**Cooking surface area determines how many servings you can actually cook at once, and it's easy to underestimate for a household of more than one or two.** A compact 2-serving grill is genuinely too small for cooking for a family or hosting; look at the square-inch spec, not just the "serves X" marketing number, since serving counts assume small portions. If you regularly cook for three or more people, a mid-size or party-size grill (roughly 130-175 sq. in.) saves real time over running multiple batches through a small one.

**A removable, dishwasher-safe cooking plate is the single biggest factor in whether you'll actually use the grill regularly.** Nonstick plates that lift out and go straight in the dishwasher get used far more often in practice than grills where you have to hand-scrub a fixed plate around a heating element — cleanup friction is the most common reason a countertop grill ends up in a cabinet after a few uses. Also check whether the nonstick coating is PFAS-free; most current-generation models from major brands have moved to PFAS-free nonstick surfaces, but it's still worth confirming on the listing rather than assuming.

## Before you buy: a quick checklist

1. **Check the actual square-inch cooking surface, not just the "serves X" number** — serving counts assume small portions and can be misleading for a household of three or more.
2. **Decide contact-press vs. open-grate/flat-surface first** — it determines both cook time and whether thick or bone-in food will actually fit.
3. **Confirm the cooking plate is removable and dishwasher-safe** before buying — this is the biggest factor in whether the grill gets used regularly or ends up in a cabinet.
4. **Check your building's specific rules**, not just general assumptions — most leases allow electric grills where they ban propane/charcoal, but a few restrict any grill-type appliance on balconies, so it's worth a quick check if you plan to use it outdoors.
5. **Confirm current price on the listing before buying** — indoor grill pricing moves with sales often, and the ranges above are approximate at the time of writing.

There's no single best indoor grill — the right one depends on how many people you're usually cooking for and whether you value speed (contact press) or an authentic sear (open flat surface) more. Any of the four picks above will get you real grilling results without breaking a lease clause: start from how many servings you actually need at once, then let that narrow the choice between a compact two-server, a smokeless mid-size grate, a multi-function griddler, or a higher-heat searing grill.
