Rowing machines are one of the few pieces of cardio equipment that work the whole body in a single motion, and unlike a treadmill or bike, most models fold up or stand on end for storage — a real advantage in a small apartment or a shared home gym corner. But "rowing machine" covers a wide range of designs, from $150 hydraulic units to $1,000+ air rowers used in national training programs, and the resistance type matters more to how it actually feels than the price tag alone. Here's what to look at before buying, and four solid picks across the budget range.

## What actually matters when choosing a rowing machine

**Resistance type.** This is the single biggest factor in how a rower feels stroke to stroke. Hydraulic rowers use piston cylinders (like a shock absorber) and are the cheapest and most compact option, but the resistance curve feels less consistent and less like real rowing — better suited to occasional, casual use than serious training. Magnetic rowers use a magnetic brake on the flywheel, giving smooth, quiet, consistent resistance at fixed levels you dial in yourself; they're the most common mid-range option and work well in apartments since there's no fan noise. Air rowers use a flywheel with fan blades, so resistance scales automatically with how hard you pull — harder effort means more resistance, closer to the feel of rowing on water — but they're louder and typically pricier. Some newer models blend air and magnetic resistance in one flywheel for a quieter version of the air-rower feel.

**Footprint and storage.** Most home rowers are 7-9 feet long when in use. Folding models (hinge in the middle) cut that down significantly and can often stand upright against a wall; look at the folded dimensions, not just "foldable," since some models fold to a size that's still awkward to tuck away. Non-folding models, like the Concept2, are generally sturdier but need a permanent 8-foot stretch of floor.

**Weight capacity and monitor.** Check the max user weight against your needs — budget hydraulic models often cap around 250 lbs, while magnetic and air models commonly go to 300-500 lbs. Every rower in this price range comes with some kind of monitor (time, distance, stroke rate, calories); higher-end models add Bluetooth sync to fitness apps, which matters if you want workout history without writing it down yourself.

**Noise.** If you're in an apartment or rowing near a sleeping household, magnetic resistance is the quietest option by a clear margin. Air rowers have a distinct whooshing fan sound that some people like (it feels more like real rowing) and others find too loud for early-morning or late-night sessions.

## Before you buy: quick checklist

- Match resistance type to your priorities: hydraulic for lowest cost and casual use, magnetic for quiet consistency, air (or hybrid) for the most realistic rowing feel.
- Check both assembled and folded dimensions against your actual space, not just whether it's labeled "foldable."
- Confirm max user weight capacity against your needs before buying, especially on budget hydraulic models.
- Prices and exact configurations (leg height, monitor version, bundled accessories) can shift between retailers — always confirm the current price and specs on the live listing before buying.
