Buying "a basketball" sounds simple until you're standing in the sporting goods aisle looking at a wall of orange spheres ranging from $15 to $100+, in sizes 5 through 7, in leather, composite, and rubber. The ball you need depends almost entirely on two things: what size fits your hand and age group, and what surface you're actually going to play on. Get either one wrong and you'll end up with a ball that slips out of your hands, wears out in a month, or just doesn't bounce right.

## Size: get this right first

Basketball size is standardized by age and gender, not by preference, so this part isn't really a style choice:

- **Size 7 (29.5")** — the official size for men's high school, college, and the NBA. Weighs about 22 oz. This is what most adult men and boys age 14+ should be using.
- **Size 6 (28.5")** — standard for women's college and professional play (WNBA, NCAA Women's), and also the recommended size for boys ages 12-14. Weighs about 20 oz.
- **Size 5 (27.5")** — the youth size for kids roughly ages 9-11. Lighter and easier for smaller hands to palm and shoot with proper form.

A ball that's too big for a young player's hands teaches bad shooting mechanics (pushing from the chest instead of snapping the wrist), so don't just buy "the big one" for a kid who's still growing into the game.

## Material: indoor, outdoor, or both

This is the bigger decision for most buyers, and it's where people get tripped up.

**Genuine/full-grain leather** is used in official indoor game balls (this is what you see in the NBA). It has the best feel and grip, but it must stay indoors — leather scuffs, dries out, and degrades quickly on asphalt or concrete. It also usually needs a short "break-in" period before it feels right.

**Composite leather** (sometimes called synthetic leather or microfiber composite) is the right call for most people. It mimics the tackiness and feel of genuine leather fairly closely, but it's more durable and far more tolerant of mixed indoor/outdoor use. If you only want to own one basketball, this is almost always the material to pick.

**Rubber** is built for abuse — driveway concrete, asphalt playgrounds, wet courts. It's the cheapest and toughest option, but the grip and feel are noticeably harder and less responsive than composite or leather, which is why most rubber balls are marketed specifically as "outdoor" or "street" balls rather than for serious skill-building play.

## What else actually matters

- **Channel depth / pebbling.** Deeper seams ("wide channel" designs) and more pronounced pebbling improve grip, especially for players with sweaty hands or for outdoor play where dust and grit reduce traction over time.
- **Bladder quality.** Butyl bladders hold air pressure longer between pumps than cheaper bladders — not a dealbreaker, but worth knowing if you're annoyed by balls that go soft every week.
- **Regulation vs. recreational.** If you care about tournament or league play, check for official approval (NFHS, NCAA) printed on the ball; recreational balls skip this without any real downside for casual games.

## How to decide

- **Play only in a gym or on a home hoop with a hard indoor-style surface?** Get a dedicated indoor composite ball (Wilson Evolution or Molten GG7X).
- **Play only outside, on asphalt or concrete?** Skip composite leather entirely and get a rubber outdoor ball — composite covers wear out fast on rough pavement, so a true outdoor/rubber build will actually last longer and cost less.
- **Split time between both, or not sure yet?** An indoor/outdoor composite ball like the Spalding Zi/O is the sensible middle ground.
- **Buying for a kid?** Match the size to age first (5 for 9-11, 6 for 12-14, 7 for 14+), and don't over-spend on a premium indoor composite ball until they're playing regularly enough to notice the difference.
