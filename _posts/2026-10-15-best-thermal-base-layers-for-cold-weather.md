A good base layer is the piece of winter gear people skip until they're standing outside shivering in a cotton t-shirt under their jacket. Unlike an insulated coat or gloves, a base layer's whole job is to sit against your skin and manage moisture while trapping a thin layer of warm air — which means fabric choice and fit matter more here than almost any other winter garment. Get it wrong (cotton, too loose, too heavy for the activity) and everything you layer on top of it works against you instead of for you.

This guide covers the real trade-offs between merino wool and synthetic fabrics, how to pick a weight class, and real picks from budget synthetic layers to premium merino wool.

## What actually matters when choosing a base layer

**Merino wool vs. synthetic.** This is the core decision. Merino wool regulates temperature well across a range of conditions, resists odor for days of wear (useful on multi-day trips when you can't do laundry), and stays warmer when damp than synthetic fabric. It's also pricier and less durable under heavy abrasion — most merino base layers are rated for roughly 200-500 days of wear before wearing thin, versus 500-1,000 days for a comparable synthetic. Synthetic base layers (usually polyester blends) are cheaper, dry faster, and hold up to repeated hard use and washing, but they pick up body odor faster and don't perform quite as well across temperature swings.

**Weight class.** Base layers are generally sold as lightweight, midweight, or heavyweight, corresponding roughly to fabric density (grams per square meter for wool, similar logic for synthetics). Lightweight suits high-output activity in cool-but-not-frigid conditions, where you don't want to overheat — running, aerobic hiking. Midweight is the most versatile all-around choice for general cold-weather wear, moderate activity, or layering under a shell. Heavyweight (often 250g/m² merino or thicker synthetic fleece-backed fabric) is built for standing around in serious cold — skiing lift lines, winter camping, very low temperatures — and will overheat you if worn during real exertion.

**Fit.** A base layer needs to sit close to the skin — not compression-tight, but without loose, baggy fabric — because its insulating and moisture-wicking function depends on consistent skin contact. Oversized base layers trap less warm air and wick moisture less effectively; sizing down from your normal size is common advice across both merino and synthetic lines.

**Moisture management.** Whatever the fabric, the base layer's job is to move sweat away from skin before it cools and chills you. This is where cotton actively fails — it absorbs moisture and holds it against skin rather than wicking it away, which is why "cotton kills" is standard advice for any cold-weather layering system, winter sports included.

**Seams and construction.** Flatlock or flat seams reduce chafing versus bulkier overlock seams, which matters more for layers worn under tight-fitting outerwear or for multi-hour activity. Thumbholes on long-sleeve tops help keep sleeves from riding up under gloves — a small detail but a genuinely useful one for skiing or cold-weather work.

## Verify before you buy

- Check the stated weight class (lightweight/midweight/heavyweight or the gsm number) against your actual activity level, not just the temperature — overheating during exertion is as uncomfortable as being cold.
- Size down if you're between sizes; a snug fit performs better than a loose one for both merino and synthetic base layers.
- If buying merino, check the wool percentage — some "merino blend" products mix in synthetic fiber for durability, which changes the odor-resistance and warmth profile somewhat.
- Avoid cotton entirely for any base layer meant for active cold-weather use or sustained outdoor exposure.
- Prices on all of these shift with seasonal sales heading into winter, so compare current listings across retailers rather than relying on any price quoted here.
