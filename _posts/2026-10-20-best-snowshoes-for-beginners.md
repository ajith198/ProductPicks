As the first real snow starts piling up, snowshoeing is one of the cheapest ways to turn a snowed-in trail into a workout instead of a dead end. Unlike a lot of winter sports gear, snowshoes don't require a lesson to use — if you can walk, you can snowshoe — but picking the wrong size or binding style for your weight and terrain can turn a nice afternoon into a slog of post-holing through snow that should have supported you.

This guide covers what actually determines whether a pair of snowshoes works for you, then real picks across budget tiers.

## What actually matters when choosing beginner snowshoes

**Sizing by total weight, not just body weight.** Snowshoe flotation is driven mostly by length, and manufacturers publish a weight capacity for each size that's meant to include your body weight *plus* clothing, boots, and anything you're carrying — not just what you'd see on a bathroom scale. MSR, one of the most established snowshoe brands, suggests adding roughly 10-15 lbs to your body weight for a simple day trip to land on the right size. Most beginners on groomed or packed trails do fine with a 21-25 inch shoe; larger 30-36 inch shoes exist mainly for deeper powder or heavier total loads.

**Snow type changes the math.** The same size snowshoe behaves differently in fresh powder versus packed trail snow — powder needs more surface area to avoid sinking, while denser, packed snow lets you get away with a smaller, lighter shoe. If you're between two sizes and expect to encounter deep or ungroomed snow, sizing up is the safer call; if you're consistently on groomed or well-traveled trails, you can lean smaller for less weight on your feet.

**Bindings.** Entry-level snowshoes typically use either a strap binding or a ratchet binding. Ratchet systems (often double-ratchet, one strap over the forefoot and one at the heel) tend to be faster to put on and take off with gloves on and distribute pressure more evenly, which matters more than it sounds like after a few miles. Strap/buckle bindings are usually cheaper and still work fine for casual, infrequent use, but they're slower to adjust and can loosen over a long outing.

**Traction (crampons).** Every snowshoe has some form of metal traction on the underside — usually a toe crampon at minimum, with better models adding side rails or a heel crampon for traversing side-slopes and descending. Beginners sticking to flat or gently rolling trails don't need aggressive mountaineering-style traction, but even moderate rolling terrain benefits from a shoe with more than just a toe crampon, since that's what keeps you from sliding sideways on a hard-packed or icy section.

**Heel lifters (televators).** These are small bars that flip up under your heel to reduce calf strain on sustained climbs. They're a genuine comfort feature on hilly terrain but unnecessary if you're mostly on flat trails — not worth paying extra for if that's your use case.

**Kits with poles.** Several brands sell a "kit" that bundles the snowshoes with trekking poles and a carry bag. If you don't already own trekking poles, a kit is usually better value than buying the pieces separately, and poles genuinely help with balance for first-timers.

## A simple decision framework

- **Just want to try it once or twice this winter, mostly flat trails** → Yukon Charlie's Advanced Float or a similarly priced budget aluminum shoe.
- **Want ratchet bindings and heel lifters without paying premium prices** → Retrospec Drifter, but check current pricing across a couple of retailers first since it fluctuates.
- **Don't already own poles and want everything in one box** → a kit (Yukon Charlie's Sherpa, Tubbs Xplore, or an MSR Evo Trail kit).
- **Plan to snowshoe regularly for multiple seasons, including hillier terrain** → MSR Evo Trail or Tubbs Xplore, both built to hold up to repeated use with better traction for side-slopes.

## Verify before you buy

- Add your actual winter clothing, boots, and any pack weight to your body weight before checking a size's weight capacity — don't size based on body weight alone.
- Check whether the listing is for a single snowshoe or a pair; some marketplace listings are ambiguous about this.
- Confirm whether poles and a carry bag are included or sold separately — "kit" listings vary by brand in what's bundled.
- Prices on budget models in particular swing widely between retailers and over the season as snow arrives in different regions, so compare a couple of current listings rather than relying on any price quoted here.
- If you'll be on anything beyond flat, groomed trails, look specifically for a model with side rails or a heel crampon, not just a toe crampon.
