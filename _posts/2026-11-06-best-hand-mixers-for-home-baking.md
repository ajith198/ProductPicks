A hand mixer sits in the gap between a whisk and a full stand mixer: it's for whoever bakes often enough to be tired of mixing cookie dough by hand, but not often enough (or doesn't have the counter space) to justify a $300+ stand mixer that lives permanently on the counter. The category runs from roughly $20 basic 5- or 6-speed mixers up to $150+ premium models with digital controls and a built-in scraper beater, and most of that price spread buys you motor durability, attachment quality, and convenience features rather than a fundamentally different mixing result — a cheap mixer and an expensive one will both whip egg whites to stiff peaks, just with different amounts of effort and noise along the way.

## What actually matters

**Speed count and low-speed control matter more than peak wattage.** Hand mixer wattage numbers (usually 150-300W) aren't standardized the way they are on stand mixers, so two mixers rated similarly can feel very different in your hand. What actually affects baking results is how many distinct speeds you have and, especially, how gentle the lowest speed is — a mixer that can't go slow enough will fling flour out of the bowl the moment you add it, while a 5-6 speed mixer with a genuinely slow first setting handles that step cleanly. More speeds (7-9) mostly add finer gradation in the middle of the range, useful for things like gradually stiffening whipped cream, but aren't essential for basic cookie and cake batters.

**Beater and whisk attachment quality determines what you can actually make, not just how fast.** Every hand mixer ships with a pair of standard beaters for batters and doughs, but the attachments that come bundled vary a lot: a wire whisk attachment is needed for whipped cream and egg whites (standard beaters aerate poorly), and some mixers add dough hooks for light bread/pizza dough. If you bake a wide variety of things, check the attachment count before buying rather than assuming "it's a hand mixer, they're all the same" — a mixer with only two beaters and no whisk will frustrate you the first time a recipe calls for whipped cream.

**A built-in scraper beater or "soft-start" feature reduces the annoying parts of hand mixing, but isn't essential.** Scraper beaters (one beater with a flexible silicone edge that scrapes the bowl wall as it spins) cut down on how often you have to stop and scrape the sides by hand — genuinely useful for frequent bakers, less important if you only bake occasionally. Soft-start ramps the motor up gradually instead of lurching to full speed the instant you press the button, which matters mainly for avoiding flour clouds when starting on a dry mix. Neither feature is make-or-break, but they're worth a small price premium if you bake several times a week.

**Cordless models trade a little power and runtime for real convenience, and are worth it for some but not most people.** A newer category of cordless/rechargeable hand mixers (led by KitchenAid's cordless line) removes the cord entirely, which is genuinely nice for maneuvering around a bowl on a crowded counter, but they run on battery charge, usually good for a reasonable number of mixing sessions before needing a recharge, and tend to cost more than a corded mixer with similar speed range. Worth considering if cord management specifically bothers you; skip it if you're optimizing purely for value.

## Before you buy: a quick checklist

1. **Check what attachments are included** — make sure a whisk (and dough hooks, if you want them) ships standard rather than being a separate purchase.
2. **Confirm the lowest speed setting is genuinely gentle** — this is what prevents flour clouds and splattering when you're just starting to combine ingredients.
3. **Consider a scraper beater only if you bake often enough to feel the benefit** — it's a nice-to-have, not essential, for occasional bakers.
4. **Decide if cordless convenience is worth the price premium for your kitchen setup** — it's a real upgrade for some counters, unnecessary for others.
5. **Confirm current price on the listing before buying** — hand mixer pricing shifts with sales and retailer, and the ranges above are approximate at the time of writing.

There's no single "best" hand mixer — the right one depends on how often you bake and how much you value small conveniences like a scraper beater or a missing cord. Any of the five picks above will whip, cream, and mix reliably: start from how frequently you're actually going to use it, and let that decide between the budget Hamilton Beach, the do-everything Cuisinart, the KitchenAid options, or the premium Breville.
