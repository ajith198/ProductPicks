Trekking poles are one of those pieces of gear that look optional right up until you're three miles into a downhill stretch with tired knees, or picking your way across a stream crossing without a free hand to catch yourself. They take a real share of the impact that would otherwise land on your legs, and the extra two points of contact make uneven terrain, loose gravel, and stream crossings noticeably more stable. For a new hiker, they're a low-cost, high-value upgrade — but the range of options, from $25 aluminum pairs to $200-plus folding carbon poles, makes it easy to either overspend on features you don't need yet or underspend on a pair that bends the first time you lean on it hard.

This guide covers what actually matters when choosing your first trekking poles, plus specific models worth considering. We haven't personally tested every pair listed here — we've researched published specs, pricing, and reviewer coverage (REI Expert Advice, CleverHiker, Backpacker, GearJunkie, Treeline Review), and we'll flag anywhere we're relying on that rather than hands-on use.

## What Actually Matters in a Trekking Pole

### Aluminum vs. Carbon Fiber

This is the biggest fork in the road, and it's mostly a weight-vs-durability-vs-price tradeoff rather than one material being objectively better. Aluminum poles typically run 18-22 oz per pair and cost noticeably less — budget aluminum pairs start around $25-$30, with well-known beginner-friendly models in the $90-$110 range. Aluminum's failure mode under heavy side load is bending, not snapping, which means a bent pole is often still usable to limp back to the trailhead. Carbon fiber poles cut weight to roughly 12-18 oz per pair, which is genuinely noticeable on long days, but they cost more (carbon pairs commonly start around $100 and run well past $200) and their failure mode under a hard shock-load or fall is more likely to be a clean snap than a bend. For a first pair of poles used on day hikes and moderate trails, aluminum's lower cost and more forgiving failure mode make it the easier recommendation; carbon earns its premium once you're covering serious mileage and weight starts to matter more than raw toughness.

### Locking Mechanism: Twist vs. Lever (Flick) Lock

Adjustable poles collapse for packing and extend for use, and how they lock at your chosen length matters more day-to-day than most buyers expect. Twist locks (an internal expander you tighten by rotating the shaft) are simple and lighter, but can slip under load if overtightened wear sets in, or under-tightened, and they're fiddlier to adjust with cold or gloved hands. Lever locks (also called flick locks or FlickLock, a clamp on the outside of the shaft) are quicker to adjust on the fly — useful for lengthening poles on descents and shortening them on climbs — and tend to hold more reliably over years of use, at a small weight and cost penalty. If you plan to actually adjust pole length as terrain changes rather than setting it once and leaving it, prioritize a lever-lock model.

### Adjustable vs. Folding (Z-Pole)

Traditional telescoping poles adjust by sliding sections and locking at your preferred length — simple, durable, and usually the cheaper construction. Folding (Z-pole) designs collapse into a compact bundle held together by an internal cord, packing down small enough to fit in a pack's side pocket or even inside the main compartment, which frequent flyers and minimalist packers tend to prefer. The tradeoff is usually a smaller adjustment range and a higher price for the folding mechanism. For a first pair used mostly for local trails and car-camping-adjacent trips, a standard adjustable telescoping pole is the simpler and cheaper starting point.

### Grip Material

Cork grips wick moisture from sweaty hands and mold slightly to your grip shape over time, which is why they show up on more expensive poles. Foam grips are lighter and cheaper, and perform fine in cold weather since they insulate better than cork, but can feel less secure when hands get sweaty in warm conditions. Rubber grips are the least common on hiking-focused poles (more typical on trail-running poles) since they transmit more vibration and heat to the hand. None of these is wrong for a beginner — it's a comfort preference more than a functional dealbreaker.

### Tip and Basket Compatibility

Poles ship with rubber tip protectors for pavement and trailhead approach walks, and carbide tips underneath for grip on rock, roots, and hardpack. Baskets (the small discs above the tip) keep the pole from sinking into mud or snow; small trekking baskets come standard, while wider snow baskets are a separate accessory worth checking for availability if you'll hike in winter. These are wear items on any pole, so before buying, it's worth a quick check that replacement tips and baskets are actually sold for the specific model.

- Confirm the pole's **adjustment range** covers your height comfortably at both extremes — shortened for steep climbs, extended for descents.

- If you'll hike in snow, confirm **wide snow baskets** are available as an add-on for the model you're considering.
