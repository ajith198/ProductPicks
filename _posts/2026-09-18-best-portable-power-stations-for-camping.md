A portable power station sits somewhere between a giant phone charger and a small home generator — a sealed battery pack with real AC outlets, USB ports, and sometimes a car socket, built to keep a phone, camp light, mini fridge, or CPAP machine running for a weekend without a fuel-burning generator's noise or fumes. The market is dominated by a handful of brands (Jackery, EcoFlow, Bluetti, Anker Solix) that all make broadly similar compact units, which makes the spec sheet more important than the logo. Here's what actually matters when picking one for camping, and a few real models worth considering.

## What actually matters

**Watt-hours (Wh), not just "battery."** Capacity is measured in watt-hours — roughly, how much total energy the unit stores. For weekend camping, most compact models fall in the 200-300Wh range, enough to charge a phone dozens of times, run a camp light for days, or keep a CPAP running for one or two nights. If you're trying to run a cooler or mini fridge for a multi-day trip, you'll want to look at larger 1,000Wh-plus units instead — this guide focuses on the compact, easy-to-pack tier.

**Continuous output (W) vs. surge/peak output.** The wattage rating tells you what the unit can actually power at once — a 300W continuous rating runs small electronics and lights fine but won't start a device with a high startup draw, like a compressor fridge or a hair dryer, even if the unit lists a higher "peak" or "surge" number for brief spikes. Check the specific device you want to run against the station's continuous rating, not just its total capacity.

**Battery chemistry: LiFePO4 vs. standard lithium-ion.** Nearly every current model in this class uses LiFePO4 (lithium iron phosphate) cells, which last for roughly 2,500-3,000+ charge cycles before capacity noticeably degrades — several times longer than older lithium-ion power stations, and safer under heat. If you see a much cheaper listing that doesn't specify LiFePO4, check the spec sheet before assuming it's the same technology.

**Charging speed.** Recent models can recharge from empty to full via AC wall outlet in under an hour, which matters if you're topping off overnight or between trips rather than waiting half a day. Solar recharging is also common but slower and dependent on panel wattage and sunlight — treat solar input as a nice-to-have for extending a trip, not a fast primary charging method.

**Weight and port selection.** In this compact 245-300Wh class, units typically weigh 6-8 lbs — genuinely easy to toss in a car trunk or pack for car camping, though still bulkier than it sounds next to a phone charger. Check that the port mix (AC outlets, USB-A, USB-C with enough wattage for laptop charging, car-style DC) matches what you'll actually plug in, since some budget models skimp on AC outlet count.

## Verify before you buy

- Confirm current price and exact capacity/output specs on the retailer's listing — these brands revise models frequently and older listings can be out of date.
- Check the continuous wattage rating against the actual device you plan to run most, not just the headline "peak" number.
- If you want solar recharging, confirm the specific solar panel wattage and connector type sold for that model — panels are usually a separate purchase.
- These compact 200-300Wh units are sized for phones, lights, and small electronics over a weekend, not for running a fridge or AC unit for days — size up to a larger-capacity model if that's your use case.
