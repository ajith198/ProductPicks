Picking a first backpacking backpack is different from picking a daypack. Get the size or fit wrong and it isn't just uncomfortable — it can mean sore hips, a straining back, or gear that won't fit for a two-night trip. The good news is that the backpacking pack market has matured enough that a genuinely solid beginner pack now runs well under $200, while packs built around premium suspension systems and load-hauling comfort can run past $300. The right choice depends far more on torso fit and how much you're carrying than on brand name.

This guide covers what actually matters when choosing a backpacking backpack, plus specific models worth considering at different price points. ## What Actually Matters in a Backpacking Backpack

### Capacity (Liters)

Backpack capacity is measured in liters of internal volume, and the right size depends on trip length, not just body size. For one- to three-night trips, most hikers do well in the 40-55 liter range. Go smaller (35-45L) if you already own lightweight gear and pack minimally; go larger (55-65L) if you're carrying a bulkier sleeping bag, a two-person tent split between packs, or extra layers for cold-weather trips. Buying the biggest pack "just in case" tends to backfire — an underfilled large pack rides worse than a properly filled smaller one.

### Torso Fit vs. Body Height

The single most common beginner mistake is buying a pack size based on height instead of torso length. Most backpacking packs come in independently adjustable torso-length ranges (often labeled S/M/L or with an adjustable hip-belt/shoulder-strap system), and a pack that's the wrong torso length will transfer weight poorly no matter how good its suspension is. Measuring torso length (from the C7 vertebra at the base of the neck to the iliac crest at hip level) and matching it to a specific manufacturer's size chart matters more than picking a pack because it "looks big enough."

### Suspension System and Hip Belt

The hip belt, not the shoulder straps, should carry the majority of a loaded pack's weight — a properly fitted hip belt sits on top of the hip bones (the iliac crest) and transfers most of the load there. Suspension systems vary from simple padded frames on budget packs to trampoline-style mesh back panels (like Osprey's Anti-Gravity system) designed to increase airflow and evenly distribute load on multi-day trips. More sophisticated suspension generally means more comfort under heavier loads, at a higher price and often a bit more pack weight.

### Load Range

Every pack has a comfortable maximum carrying weight — usually published in the spec sheet as a load range (e.g., "supports loads up to 40 lbs"). A pack rated for a lower load range can still work fine if you pack light, but pushing well past a pack's rated load range is where hip belts and frames start to feel inadequate regardless of brand.

### Pack Weight

There's a real tradeoff between suspension comfort/features and the pack's own base weight. Simpler, lighter-frame packs shave off a pound or more compared to fully-suspended comfort packs, which matters over a multi-day trip but usually comes with a less forgiving ride under heavier loads. Beginners carrying standard (non-ultralight) gear generally do better prioritizing suspension comfort over shaving pack ounces.

## A Few Things to Verify Before You Buy

- Measure your actual torso length and check it against the specific pack's size chart — don't size a backpacking pack off your height or off a daypack you already own.
- Confirm the pack's rated load range against how much gear you actually expect to carry, including food and water.
- Try to load the pack (or a similar demo unit) before committing if you can — hip belt fit varies by body shape in ways spec sheets don't fully capture.
- Check whether the size/capacity you're considering is a men's, women's, or unisex fit, since torso and hip belt shaping differ across those lines even within the same model name.
- Look up the **current price directly on the retailer's page** — backpacking pack prices shift with seasonal sales and model-year transitions more than the sticker price alone suggests.
