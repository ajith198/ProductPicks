A heated vest solves a specific problem that a regular winter coat doesn't: keeping your core warm without bulking up your arms, which matters if you're working with your hands, walking a dog, tailgating, or doing anything else where a full heated jacket gets in the way. The category has converged on a few clear designs — lightweight fleece or puffer vests with their own small rechargeable battery, and heavier work-oriented vests that run off the same battery as a cordless power tool — and the right one depends mostly on how many heating zones you actually need and whether you already own a battery platform you can reuse.

## What actually matters

**Heating zone count and placement matter more than wattage specs you can't easily compare.** Most consumer heated vests use carbon-fiber or copper heating panels sewn into the chest, upper back, and lower back, with four-zone designs (two front, two rear) being the current standard on mid-range models. Fewer zones means more cold spots, especially around the shoulders and sides; some premium models add collar or side-panel heating, which is worth paying for if you're outside for hours rather than running between a car and a building.

**Battery voltage and capacity drive both runtime and how hot the vest actually gets.** Dedicated heated-apparel batteries are commonly rated around 7.4V with capacities in the 4,000-5,000mAh range, which typically nets 3-4 hours on the highest of 3 heat settings and up to around 8-10 hours on low. These batteries usually charge over USB-C and can double as a small power bank for a phone in a pinch. Vests built on a cordless-tool battery platform (DEWALT's 20V Max system is the best-known example) run noticeably hotter and longer because they're pulling from a much larger battery, but that battery is heavier, bulkier, and only makes sense if you already own that tool brand's chargers and packs.

**Washability is a real practical difference, not a footnote.** Most heated vests need the battery and sometimes the heating-panel wiring harness removed before washing, and manufacturers generally recommend gentle/hand-wash cycles rather than a regular machine cycle to protect the heating elements and wiring. A vest that's awkward to disconnect is one you'll end up not washing as often as you should.

**Fit should be sized slightly roomier than your normal size**, since heated vests are usually worn as a mid-layer over a base layer and under a shell or coat in genuinely cold conditions, and a too-snug fit can also compress the heating panels against your body less evenly.

## Before you buy: a quick checklist

1. **Count the heating zones and note their placement** — four zones (chest x2, back x2) is the current standard; fewer means more cold spots.
2. **Check battery voltage, capacity, and the runtime at the heat setting you'll actually use**, not just the "up to X hours" headline figure, which almost always refers to the lowest setting.
3. **Confirm how the battery and wiring harness detach for washing** before buying, and check whether the manual calls for hand-wash only.
4. **If you already own a cordless-tool battery platform (DEWALT, Milwaukee, etc.), check whether that brand makes a heated vest** — it can be cheaper in the long run if you already have spare batteries and chargers.
5. **Size up slightly if you plan to layer the vest under a coat**, and verify current price and size availability on the listing, since heated-apparel pricing swings significantly around seasonal sales.

There's no single "best" heated vest — the right one depends on how much heat and runtime you need versus how much you want to spend, and whether you're already invested in a tool-battery ecosystem. Any of the five picks above will genuinely keep your core warm: start from your budget and how extreme the cold you're dealing with actually is, and let that decide between the budget SNOW LEOPARD, the widely-recommended ORORO or Venustas fleece vests, the tool-battery DEWALT, or the insulated Venustas down vest.
