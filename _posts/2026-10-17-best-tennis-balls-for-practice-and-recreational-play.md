Tennis balls look interchangeable on a store shelf, but the can you grab actually changes how long your balls last, how fast they play, and whether they're even right for your court surface. Buying the wrong duty rating is the single most common mistake recreational players make — and it's an easy one to avoid once you know what the can is telling you.

## What actually matters

**Felt duty: regular vs. extra.** This is the most important spec on the can, and it's tied to court surface, not skill level. Extra-duty balls have thicker, more durable felt built to survive the abrasive surface of hard courts (asphalt or concrete under an acrylic coating) — the tradeoff is they play a touch slower once the nap fluffs up. Regular-duty balls have thinner, tighter felt designed for clay and indoor/carpet courts, where there's less surface abrasion; put a regular-duty ball on a hard court and the surface will often strip the felt within a few sets. If you're not sure what you're playing on, extra-duty is the safer default for most US recreational players, since the large majority of public and club courts are hard courts.

**Pressurized vs. pressureless.** Pressurized balls — the kind used in actual matches — are filled with pressurized gas that gives them their lively bounce and are the standard for match play, but they start losing pressure (and bounce) the moment the can is opened, typically going noticeably "dead" within a few weeks of regular use even if stored in the can. Pressureless balls have solid rubber cores instead of gas, so they don't go dead the same way — they're denser and feel heavier at first, and the bounce can take a little breaking-in, but a can of pressureless balls can last many times longer than pressurized ones. For a ball machine, a backboard, or casual hitting where consistent bounce matters less than longevity, pressureless is usually the smarter buy.

**Court surface and conditions.** Clay (including the crushed-shell courts common at many parks and clubs) tends to be kinder to regular-duty felt, since the issue there is dust and moisture absorption rather than abrasion — thick felt soaks up grit and gets heavy. If you play at elevation (roughly 3,500+ feet), look for balls specifically labeled "high altitude," which are built with a different internal pressure to compensate for thinner air; using standard balls at altitude makes them fly noticeably faster and harder to control.

**Can size and value.** Tennis balls are sold in 3-ball cans, bulk cases (12-24 cans), and bags of loose balls (18-100+) without the pressurized can at all — bag-packed balls are nearly always pressureless, since there's no can to hold pressure in. If you play often, a bulk case of pressurized cans or a large bag of pressureless balls both work out cheaper per ball than buying single 3-packs repeatedly.

## A simple way to decide

- **Playing on a hard court (most common):** extra-duty, pressurized, from Penn, Wilson, or Dunlop's Championship lines.
- **Playing on clay or an indoor/carpet court:** regular-duty felt will last longer and play truer than extra-duty on these surfaces.
- **Practicing alone, using a ball machine, or outfitting kids/beginners:** pressureless, bought in bulk, to avoid the cost and waste of pressurized cans going dead.
- **Playing at elevation above ~3,500 feet:** look specifically for a "high altitude" label, not just extra-duty.

## Before you buy, verify

- Confirm the duty rating (regular vs. extra) matches your actual court surface, not just whatever's cheapest on the shelf.
- If buying pressurized cans, check the can is sealed — a hissing pop when opened confirms the pressure hasn't already leaked out on the shelf.
- For bulk bags, check whether the listing is pressurized or pressureless — some sellers mix terminology, and it changes how long the balls will stay playable.
- Prices above are approximate and shift with retailer promotions — check the current listing before buying, especially for multi-can cases where per-ball pricing varies a lot.
